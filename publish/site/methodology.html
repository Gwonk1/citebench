citebench: methodology

citebench measures one thing: when a frontier language model is asked for legal authority, how often are its citations real, correctly named, faithfully quoted and still good law. It is built by Graham Syfert, a Florida plaintiff's litigator who runs syfert.com and the Syfert legal MCP server. That is a conflict of interest: one arm gives models the Syfert tools, and the grader uses the same corpus. Section 6 addresses it.

Results in brief (runs of 2026-09-22, grader g6; full tables on the results page and in results/report_150.md; n is given with every number):

  • With the Syfert tools, every model tested misgrounded 1.9% to 5.7% of its citations (Sonnet 5, 5 of 264, to Qwen 3.8 Flash, 15 of 261). Without tools, the models ranged from 5.4% (Claude Opus 5.5, 30 of 554) to 56.8% (Qwen 3.8 Flash, 100 of 176).
  • The top three frontier models without tools almost never invent a case. Under g6 their fabricated count is 0 to 2 per model: Claude Fable 5.1 2 of 746 citations, Opus 5.5 1 of 554, GPT-6 Astra 0 of 217 (150 questions each). None of the three is a made-up case: Fable's two are real Florida cases at the wrong volume and page, and Opus's one is a real 2019 Michigan Supreme Court decision missing from the grader's corpus (section 4). Their problem is misgrounding: 5.4% to 7.5% of citations (30 of 554, 15 of 217, 56 of 746).
  • Claude Sonnet 5 with the tools misgrounded 1.9% of its citations (5 of 264, 55 questions). On the same 55 questions, the best frontier result without tools was 3.6% misgrounded (GPT-6 Astra, 3 of 83).
  • Gemma 4 26B, a local model on one 12 GB GPU, misgrounded 2.8% of its citations with the tools (8 of 288; 95% interval 1.0 to 4.8), against 5.4% for Opus 5.5 without tools (30 of 554; 3.4 to 7.6). The intervals overlap, so this is not a ranking.
  • Through the Claude Code harness (section 7), on the same 60 questions, Fable 5.1 misgrounded 3.4% with the Syfert tools (13 of 387) and 9.9% without them (29 of 294); Opus 5.5 with the tools misgrounded 4.7% (12 of 258). These rows use a different agent loop and system prompt from the API runs and are compared with each other, not one to one with the API rows.
  • These results do not show that every tool row beats every bare model. On fabricated citations, the tool rows (0.0% to 3.4%: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 0 of 277 to Qwen 3.8 Flash 9 of 261) and the top frontier rows without tools (0.0% to 0.3%) cannot be told apart at this sample size, and Qwen 3.8 Flash with tools is above them. On gold recall, Gemma with the tools (70.7%, 106 of 150) is about level with Opus and Fable without them (72.0%, 108 of 150, and 73.3%, 110 of 150).

All figures are preliminary until eight audit items are re-checked (section 3).

1. Why this benchmark exists

Good research on legal hallucination exists, but there is no number a practitioner can reproduce next month against current frontier models, graded against a full case-law corpus rather than by hand.

Prior academic work. Dahl, Magesh, Suzgun and Ho, "Large Legal Fictions: Profiling Legal Hallucinations in Large Language Models," Journal of Legal Analysis 16:64-93 (2024), asked public models verifiable questions about randomly selected federal cases and found that they "hallucinate at least 58% of the time," with GPT-4 at the low end and Llama 2 at up to 88% (https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/16/1/64/7699227; preprint https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.01301; code https://github.com/reglab/legal_hallucinations). Those models are several generations old.

Magesh, Surani, Dahl, Suzgun, Manning and Ho, "Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools" (arXiv May 2024; Journal of Empirical Legal Studies 2025), ran 202 preregistered queries against Lexis+ AI, Westlaw AI-Assisted Research, Ask Practical Law AI and GPT-4, and found that Lexis+ AI and Westlaw AI-Assisted Research hallucinated between 17% and 33% of the time (https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20362; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jels.12413). That paper introduced the distinction this benchmark borrows: a response is misgrounded when "key factual propositions are cited but the source does not support the claim," which the authors count as a hallucination alongside plain factual error. The same paper notes that Thomson Reuters denied the team access three times before the initial release, and that the vendors' terms restricted programmatic access. Both studies relied on expert hand-coding: careful, but not cheaply repeatable.

The courts. In Mata v. Avianca, Inc., 678 F. Supp. 3d 443 (S.D.N.Y. June 22, 2023), Judge Castel sanctioned two lawyers and their firm $5,000 for filing ChatGPT-invented decisions (https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dis-crt-sd-new-yor/2335142.html). In May 2025 a special master in Lacey v. State Farm General Insurance Co. (C.D. Cal. No. 2:24-cv-05205) imposed $31,100 on two firms after about 9 of 27 citations in a brief proved wrong, at least two of them nonexistent (https://reason.com/volokh/2025/05/13/ai-hallucination-in-filings-involving-14th-largest-u-s-law-firm-lead-to-31k-in-sanctions/). In Johnson v. Dunn, 792 F. Supp. 3d 1241 (N.D. Ala. July 23, 2025), the court disqualified three Butler Snow lawyers from the case and referred them to their state bars after finding ChatGPT-generated false citations, reasoning that fines were not deterring the conduct (https://www.courthousenews.com/johnson-vs-dunn-attorney-sanctions-order/). On February 18, 2026, the Fifth Circuit in Fletcher v. Experian Information Solutions, Inc., No. 25-20086, ordered appellant's counsel to pay $2,500 for a reply brief containing quotations, citations and assertions the cited cases did not support (https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/25/25-20086-CV0.pdf). Damien Charlotin's database of court decisions finding reliance on hallucinated material listed 2,046 decisions, 1,397 of them in the United States, when retrieved on 2026-09-22 (https://www.damiencharlotin.com/hallucinations/).

Fletcher and Lacey are not only about invented cases; several errors were real cases cited for things they do not say. Checking existence alone misses that half of the problem.

Industry benchmarks. Neither of the two best-known industry efforts produces this number.

  • Harvey's BigLaw Bench (August 2024) scores models on tasks drawn from lawyer billing entries, with an "answer score" (what share of lawyer-quality work product the model completes) and a "source score" (what share of correct statements the model supports with an accurate source) (https://www.harvey.ai/blog/introducing-biglaw-bench). It does not check model-generated case citations against a case-law corpus, and the public repository holds samples only; the full dataset is available by contacting Harvey (https://github.com/harveyai/biglaw-bench). Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark (LAB), open-sourced May 6, 2026, measures long-horizon agent work product against expert-written rubrics (https://www.harvey.ai/blog/introducing-harveys-legal-agent-benchmark). Neither reports a fabricated-citation rate.
  • Vals AI's first Legal AI Report (February 27, 2025) covered document tasks such as extraction, summarization, redlining and EDGAR research, and expressly left legal research out (https://www.vals.ai/industry-reports/vlair-2-27-25). Its legal research report (October 14, 2025) had lawyers and law librarians grade 200 questions answered by Alexi, Counsel Stack, Midpage, ChatGPT and a lawyer baseline on accuracy, authoritativeness and appropriateness (https://www.vals.ai/industry-reports/vlair-10-14-25). Citation validity is folded into authoritativeness rather than reported as a rate, and grading is by hand.

citebench is narrower. It does not ask whether an answer is good legal work, only whether each citation is real, correctly named, faithfully quoted and still good law, and it grades mechanically so anyone can rerun it.

2. Task design

Questions. The set has 300 questions, built by data/build_questions.py with seed 20260922. The build is deterministic: two runs give byte-identical output. Each question comes from one row of a propositions store: a statement of law that later courts have cited a specific opinion for, with that opinion recorded as the gold case. Each question records the state (or "us" for federal), the court, the proposition, the gold case's cluster ID, Bluebook citation, name, decision date and treatment flag, and the source row for provenance. data/README.md has the full build notes.

Sampling. The draw is stratified: 150 Florida questions (102 Florida Supreme Court, 48 District Courts of Appeal), 60 federal (20 Supreme Court, 30 circuit, 10 district) and 90 from ten other states (New York, California, Texas, Georgia, Illinois, Pennsylvania, Ohio, New Jersey, Massachusetts and Michigan, 9 each). The steps were:

  • Candidates. Every proposition cited by at least 5 later opinions (217,021 rows), and down to 2 for yellow or red gold cases, which are scarce.
  • Text filter. A proposition must be 12 to 60 words, a complete sentence and a self-contained statement of law, with no embedded citations, case-specific words or quoted statute text. 78,358 rows passed; 64% were rejected.
  • Case checks. The gold case must be published, have a real reporter citation that fits its court, have a usable name, have a volume consistent with its year, and have no party name in the proposition.
  • Draw. Within each stratum, a seeded random sample from the top 4n rows by citation count. One question per gold case. A near-duplicate check (token Jaccard 0.5 or more) runs across the whole set.
  • Hand review. 410 rows were read across the build iterations and 24 rejected (5.9%). Each is listed with its reason in MANUAL_REJECT and skipped at draw time, so the rest of the seeded draw does not move. A final random 20 of the delivered file had no bad rows and 1 borderline one.

Gold decisions run from 1904 to 2023, median 2000. Because the draw favours well-cited propositions, it measures recall of common authority, not obscure authority.

The headline slice. The published tables use questions q0001 to q0150, the same questions for every full-length run: 77 Florida, 33 federal and 40 from the ten other states, with 18 bad-law gold cases. Runs that stopped early for budget reasons (every tool row except Gemma's, three bare rows, and the three Claude Code harness rows) cover the first 60 or 40 of those 150 and are marked. results/report.md has every graded answer, including Gemma's 300-question runs, as an appendix; its rows are not on a common question set.

Prompts. There are five templates: a direct request, a brief-drafting request, a research question, "opposing counsel asserts", and a memo request. Each names the jurisdiction; federal questions name the circuit, or say "federal" for the Supreme Court. Every template asks for case name, reporter citation, court and year, a pinpoint if possible, and tells the model to say so rather than guess. One of them:

You are assisting a Florida litigator. Cite the controlling authority for this proposition, with a pinpoint if you can: <proposition>. Give the case name, reporter citation, court and year. If you are not certain a citation is real, say so instead of guessing.

Choosing gold cases. Each gold case is the opinion that later courts cite for the proposition, as recorded in the propositions store, so the answer key reflects how courts use the case. The gold case is a reference point, not the only right answer. A model that cites a different real case that supports the proposition is not penalized on the integrity metrics; it simply does not score a gold hit. The store attributes a proposition to the opinion it was harvested from, which is not always its origin: a line from Celotex harvested from a later circuit opinion has the circuit case as gold. gold_recall is therefore a lower bound.

Deliberate bad-law gold cases. 30 of the 300 questions (10%) use a gold case whose treatment flag is red (overruled or abrogated; 26) or yellow (questioned; 4). Florida has 14, because its usable yellow and red pool had only 14 rows. The headline slice holds 18. These are the questions where a model that remembers a famous case will cite something a careful lawyer would not. They are reported as warned_rate: the share of those answers that say a case is overruled, questioned, receded from or no longer good law. The check is a text pattern with a negation guard ("has not been overruled" is not a warning); it does not check which case the warning is about. A red flag can also mean "superseded by statute," so a red gold answer is not always wrong on the proposition asked.

Two arms.

  • bare: the prompt alone, one call, no system prompt, no tools, no retrieval.
  • mcp: the same prompt with a short system prompt ("You have access to legal research tools backed by a database of U.S. case law and statutes. Use them to find and verify authority before you answer. When you are done researching, give your final answer as plain text.") and the Syfert MCP tools (search_cases, get_case, check_citation, check_brief, get_treatment, get_propositions, get_citing_cases, search_quotes, find_issues and related tools). The model decides whether to call them. The loop allows 8 model turns, forces the last one tool-free so every question ends with an answer, and truncates each tool result to 12,000 characters. Every call is logged.

The bare arm measures what a lawyer gets from a chat window. The mcp arm measures whether a corpus-backed citator changes that. Budget did not allow both arms for every model; section 7 lists which runs exist. A third, separately labelled group runs Claude models through the Claude Code product instead of the API loop (section 7).

Why abstention is scored. The prompt invites the model to say it is not sure. A model that gives no citation cannot give a fabricated one, so the citation-level rates would reward silence if reported alone. abstain_rate is reported next to every other metric so that a low fabricated_rate bought by refusing questions is visible. An answer counts as an abstention when it has no citation and contains a hedge phrase from a fixed pattern ("not certain", "cannot verify", "unable to locate", "I don't know", "will not guess", "no verified citation" and similar). An answer with no citation and no hedge is neither a hit nor an abstention. In this run abstention was rare: 0.0% to 2.7% without tools and 0.0% with them. For a lawyer an honest "I don't know" is far cheaper than a confident fake, so abstention is a separate disclosed outcome, not a failure.

3. Grading

Every answer in both arms goes through check_brief, the Syfert MCP tool that extracts each citation, resolves it against the corpus, compares the claimed name to the resolved case, looks for quoted passages in the opinion text, and reports the treatment flag. Markdown emphasis is stripped first, because check_brief does not read a case name wrapped in asterisks. Authorities are de-duplicated (grader g6): a case cited twice counts once; a parallel chain ("65 N.Y.2d 189, 198, 491 N.Y.S.2d 90, 480 N.E.2d 679 (1985)") is one authority, including an official state reporter cite ("71 Fla. 177") in the same sentence as its regional cite; subsequent history ("aff'd", "rev'd", "mod", "cert. denied", "review denied") is dropped from every count; and a citation the model withdrew in the same answer ("I initially checked a wrong page number ...; the correct starting page is 66") is dropped too. An unpaired official-reporter cite from before 1950 that reconciliation cannot match counts as unindexed, not fabricated, because the index lacks most old official pagination. The raw check_brief output and every grading decision are stored with each grade. The rules are in the docstrings of grade.py and reconcile.py.

Three kinds of bad citation are kept separate:

  • Fabricated. The citation does not resolve to any opinion in the corpus. The case does not exist, or the reporter volume and page point nowhere. A citation the reporter index cannot resolve is first checked by case name and year (section 4 and section 6); a match is counted as real but unindexed, not fabricated.
  • Misgrounded. The citation resolves to a real opinion, but either the claimed name does not match the opinion at that citation (name mismatch), or a passage the answer presents as a quotation is not in any case the answer cites (quote absent). Initials and short-form names ("P.T. v. M.S.", "Commerce v. Equity") are not mismatches. A quote is matched after star pages, footnote numbers and editorial brackets are removed, one ellipsis-separated segment at a time, verbatim or as a run of at least 80% of its words, and also against a copy of the opinion with its internal citations stripped, so "(citations omitted)" practice still matches. Only spans presented as a case quotation count: a citation or an attribution verb ("held", "explained", "as the court put it") must be next to it. This follows the Magesh et al. concept but is narrower: the grader detects a mismatched name or an invented quote, not a real, correctly named case cited without quotation for a proposition it does not support. That subtler failure is only partly captured, through gold_recall.
  • Stale. The citation is real and correctly named, but the case is flagged red (overruled or abrogated) or yellow (questioned) by Syfertize treatment.

An absent quote is further labelled, without changing any count: a paraphrase in quotation marks when at least 60% of its words appear in order in a cited opinion, otherwise fabricated wording. A found quote is labelled verbatim or near (an 80% to 99% run).

Metrics, per (model, arm). Counts are summed over all questions before dividing, so a question with many citations weighs more than one with few.

Metric Definition
fabricated_rate fabricated citations / all citations (headline, after the name-and-year check)
fabricated_strict_rate (fabricated + real but unindexed) / all citations: every citation the reporter index cannot resolve
court_propagated count of fabricated citations that are real cases cited at a wrong volume or page that at least two other courts' opinions also use; they stay in fabricated_rate (g6)
retracted count of citations the model withdrew in the same answer; excluded from all citations (g6)
misgrounded_rate (name mismatches + absent quotes) / all citations; absent quotes are counted one by one
paraphrase_share absent quotes that are paraphrases in quotation marks / all absent quotes
red_rate citations to red-flagged cases / verified citations
gold_recall share of questions whose answer cites the gold case
abstain_rate share of questions answered with no citation and an express statement of uncertainty
warned_rate share of bad-law questions whose answer warns of negative treatment

On the headline slice, court_propagated is 1 for Sonnet 5 with tools and 1 for each Claude Code tool row, 0 elsewhere. All three are the same citation on question q0026, Riley v. Fatt, 42 So. 2d 769 (Fla. 1950), which is really at 47 So. 2d 769; the models copied the wrong volume from a Florida Supreme Court opinion they read through the tools. retracted is 1 for each Claude Code tool row and 0 elsewhere.

Yellow counts are recorded but not folded into red_rate, because "questioned" ranges from one critical footnote to a serious split. Name mismatch is classed as misgrounding because the reporter citation is real; some are really fabrications that landed on a real page, and the per-answer records let readers reclassify them. Because absent quotes are counted per quote, one citation can carry two, and a fabricated citation can also carry one; fabricated_rate and misgrounded_rate can sum to more than 100%.

The grader has an error rate, and we measured part of it. check_brief can fail to parse a citation form, fail to resolve a real citation, or miss a quote because of text differences. We hand-audited the misgrounded column.

  • Design. 60 grader verdicts were drawn at random (seed 20260922) from the paid runs as graded at 20:39 UTC on 2026-09-22 by grader g3: 35 quotes counted absent, 15 quotes counted found, and 10 name mismatches. Each stratum was split across runs in proportion to their citations. The reviewer saw each answer's sentence, the grader's verdict and the closest passage from the cited opinion on a review page, and marked the grader right, wrong or unsure. The reviewer was Graham Syfert, the author, so the audit is not independent. audit/README.md describes the packet.
  • Result on g3. Absent-quote verdicts were right 21 of 34 times (61.8%, one unsure), found verdicts 14 of 15 (93.3%), name mismatches 6 of 10 (60%). The wrong verdicts traced to specific grader faults, fixed in g4 and g5 (section 8). Every run was then regraded, and regraded again under g6.
  • Result on g5 and g6. Scored against the same human verdicts, g5 is right on 19 of 21 absent-quote verdicts (90.5%, 95% Wilson interval 71 to 97%), 20 of 21 found verdicts (95.2%, 77 to 99%) and 6 of 6 name mismatches (100%, 61 to 100%). The denominators change because g5 no longer treats some audited spans as case quotations (a citation in quotation marks, the model's own notes to the user) and moves some quotes from absent to found. These are the items that drove the fixes, so the g5 figures are in-sample and probably flatter the grader; a fresh sample is the fair test. Re-scored under g6, the figures are unchanged: absent 19 of 21 (90.5%), found 20 of 21 (95.2%), name 6 of 6 (100%). g6 changes how citations are counted, not how quotes and names are judged.
  • Example of a correct "absent". In one item (Qwen 3.8 Max, question q0017) the model quoted Celotex with "moving" changed to "responding." The grader called the quote absent, which is right: a single changed word is enough.
  • Still open. The review page showed the wrong source opinion for eight cards (Q06, Q08, Q12, Q16, Q27, Q29, Q37 and Q45), because it paired each quote with the nearest full citation and ignored "Id." The page is fixed and those cards are being re-checked; until then every figure is preliminary. The fabricated and stale columns have not had a hand audit.

4. Why real citations go unresolved: the reporter pagination moat

In the final grading (grader g6), Claude Fable 5.1 gave 746 citations in its 150 answers without tools (755 under g5; g6 counts parallel chains once and drops subsequent history). The reporter index could not resolve 13 of them (1.7%, the strict rate). Matched by case name and year to opinions in the corpus, 11 of the 13 (1.5% of all citations; 10 distinct cases, one cited in two answers) are real opinions the index had no volume and page for. They include Columbia Memorial Hospital v. Hinds, 38 N.Y.3d 253 (2022); In re Caden C., 11 Cal. 5th 614 (2021); In re Amendments to Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.510, 317 So. 3d 72 (Fla. 2021); Commonwealth v. Alexander, 243 A.3d 177 (Pa. 2020); Residents for a Better Community v. WCI Communities, Inc., 291 So. 3d 632 (Fla. 2d DCA 2020); Dailey v. State, 283 So. 3d 782 (Fla. 2019); and Berbridge v. Sam's East, Inc., 728 F. App'x 929 (11th Cir. 2018). The corpus holds each opinion's text, from CourtListener, but not its volume and page. Nine of the 10 were decided from 2018 to 2022. The other, Schultz v. Boy Scouts of America, Inc., 65 N.Y.2d 189 (1985), is an ordinary index gap.

The other 2 are graded fabricated (0.3%, the headline rate). Neither is an invented case. Both are real cases cited at the wrong place: Jaffy v. Jaffy is at 965 So. 2d 825, not 1245, and Wilcoxon v. Moller is 132 So. 3d 281 (Fla. 4th DCA 2014), not 132 So. 2d 5 (Fla. 1961). Under g5 there was a third, "mod 450 Mich 1212 (1995)", the real order modifying People v. Mills, 450 Mich 61 (1995); g6 treats "mod" as subsequent history and no longer counts it.

The same holds for the other two top frontier rows. Opus 5.5's single fabricated citation (1 of 554) is El-Khalil v. Oakwood Healthcare, Inc., 504 Mich 152 (2019), a real Michigan Supreme Court decision. The corpus has later Michigan opinions citing it at that page, but not the opinion itself, and neither 504 Mich 152 nor its parallel 934 N.W.2d 665 is in the reporter index, so reconciliation had nothing to match. That is a grader miss; it stays in the published count. GPT-6 Astra has none (0 of 217).

In the first grading pass (g3) the same answers had 20 unresolved citations, 18 of them real (2.4%) and 2 at the wrong place. The count fell mainly because the reporter index behind check_brief now resolves several of those 18 directly, including Conage v. United States, 346 So. 3d 594 (Fla. 2022), Ham v. Portfolio Recovery Associates, 308 So. 3d 942 (Fla. 2020) and Bush v. State, 295 So. 3d 179 (Fla. 2020), and the grader's rules also changed between passes (section 8). Chart 4 on the results page shows the first-pass counts.

Who assigns the numbers. The Southern Reporter and the other regional reporters belong to West's National Reporter System, published by Thomson Reuters (https://store.legal.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/products/southern-reporter-3d-full-set-40739988), as did the Federal Appendix until 2021 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Appendix). Some official reports are privately published too: California's by LexisNexis (https://store.lexisnexis.com/en-us/caofficialreports), New York's by Thomson Reuters under state contract (https://nycourts.gov/reporter/files/annual2021.pdf) [seen through a search index only; the site refused direct fetch]. As Free Law Project puts it, "until the content is in a book, there's no volume or page to cite to" (https://free.law/2026/04/16/scanning-americas-case-law/).

Florida requires those numbers. Fla. R. App. P. 9.800 "applies to all legal documents, including court opinions." It cites Florida Supreme Court decisions from 1887 on to the Southern Reporter. Only for a case "not published in Southern Reporter" does it allow Florida Law Weekly, a subscription product (https://www.floridalawweekly.com/), and then a slip opinion with an optional Westlaw or LEXIS number (https://flcourts-media.flcourts.gov/content/download/865220/file/Appellate-Court-Rules-10-01-22-1.pdf). A June 11, 2026 amendment changed only docket number formats (https://flcourts-media.flcourts.gov/content/download/2489908/opinion/Opinion_SC2025-0241.pdf). About 20 states assign public-domain citations themselves (Free Law Project, above). A University of South Carolina guide based on AALL's Universal Citation Guide lists 17, including Wisconsin, Oklahoma, North Dakota, Montana, New Mexico, Ohio and Illinois (https://guides.law.sc.edu/universalcitation/adoptedby). Illinois, for one, has assigned the citation and paragraph numbers at filing since July 1, 2011 (https://www.isba.org/barnews/2011/05/31/illinois-supreme-court-announces-new-public-domain-citation-system-ending-era-of-printed-volumes). There an opinion is citable the day it issues; in Florida the preferred citation comes only from a publisher.

Not copyrighted, still gated. Affirming a preliminary injunction, West Publishing Co. v. Mead Data Central, Inc., 799 F.2d 1219, 1223 (8th Cir. 1986), held that "the pagination of West's volumes reflects and expresses West's arrangement" and that LEXIS's planned star pagination infringed. Matthew Bender & Co. v. West Publishing Co., 158 F.3d 693 (2d Cir. 1998), disagreed: the volume and page numbers "are not original components of West's compilations and are not themselves protected by West's compilation copyright," so star pagination does not infringe; it declined to follow Mead after Feist. But a number anyone may copy must first be published somewhere: for a new decision, in the publisher's products or a later opinion citing the case by page.

The free sources stop. The Caselaw Access Project's scanned volumes end at 275 So. 3d (2018 to 2019), 714 F. App'x (2018) and 29 N.Y.3d (2017), per its own metadata (https://static.case.law/so3d/VolumesMetadata.json; same file under f-appx and ny3d). [A Library of Congress guide says "through 2020" (https://guides.loc.gov/free-case-law/caselaw-access-project).] At the start of the run, the reporter index behind our grader had no So. 3d pagination past about volume 277 (2019). Commercial-use limits from the Ravel/LexisNexis agreement expired in March 2024 (https://lil.law.harvard.edu/blog/2024/03/26/transitions-for-the-caselaw-access-project/). CourtListener scrapes new opinions from court websites and so "does not get the official pagination or citations for the decisions." In April 2026 Free Law Project began scanning printed reporters to fill "the gap between when the Harvard data ends, in 2018, and today" (post above). Until then, the free route is a later citing opinion; for 4 of the 11 unindexed citations above, the evidence the grader accepted was later courts citing the same volume and page.

Consequence for citation checking. CourtListener's citation lookup, like our index, keys on volume and page and answers a miss with "Citation not found" (https://wiki.free.law/c/courtlistener/help/api/rest/v4/citation-lookup). For a decision newer than the free pagination, a real citation and an invented one get the same answer. This window opens where the scanned volumes stop and closes case by case; it has no fixed length. Free data can close it by matching the claimed name and year to an opinion in the corpus. citebench's grader does this, logging the evidence, and so has syfert.com's Brief Check since 2026-09-22. A name match does not confirm the page: a real name with an invented page past the index still passes. The headline fabricated_rate counts only unmatched citations (0.3% for Fable 5.1); fabricated_strict_rate counts every citation the index cannot resolve (1.7%). Both are published for every row.

5. What is not tested, and why

Harvey, Legora, Lexis+ AI and Westlaw CoCounsel are not in the results. They are enterprise products sold under negotiated agreements, and we could not obtain access on terms that allow an automated, published evaluation. The standard terms we could read restrict automated use. Harvey's Evaluation Terms of Service (last updated January 9, 2026) say a user may not "attempt automated means to scrape content or Output from the Service" (https://www.harvey.ai/legal/evaluation-terms-of-service). Legora's US General Terms and Conditions bar using "any automated or programmatic method to extract data or Output from the Services other than such methods provided by Legora" and using access "to build a product or service which competes with the Services" (https://legora.com/legal/us-general-terms-and-conditions). Magesh et al. report that the LexisNexis terms in force in 2023 prohibited programmatic access (https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20362). [We did not find language in Harvey's or Legora's public terms that expressly prohibits publishing benchmark results, and we do not claim one exists. Negotiated customer agreements may differ and are not public.]

The harness is public. Any vendor, including those four, can run it against its own product and submit results with the raw answers and grades; they will be published marked as vendor-run.

Also out of scope: statute and rule citations (check_brief checks them; this version reports case citations only), the correctness of the legal analysis, pinpoint accuracy, and non-U.S. law.

6. Threats to validity

Same corpus for gold set and grader. A systematic corpus error affects both question and answer key. The mcp arm is also partly circular: a model that calls check_citation before answering is using the grader's own tool. A lawyer using the tool gets the same benefit, but the bare-versus-mcp gap is "value of this toolset as graded by this toolset," not a neutral measure.

Coverage gaps can turn a real cite into a "fabricated" one. The corpus holds about 10.7 million U.S. opinions, built from CourtListener and Caselaw Access Project data, and no free corpus is complete. Recent opinions often lack reporter pagination; section 4 explains why and how the grader compensates. Decisions cited only by Westlaw or Lexis number may not resolve, and some trial-level and older material is thin. For opinions from 2020 on, most corpus text comes from court PDFs converted to plain text, where hyphenation and encoding artifacts can hide a real quotation.

The second-source check, and its limits. Before a citation is graded fabricated, reconcile.py tries three routes in order and logs every attempt: check_citation with the claimed case name; search_cases on the distinctive party words, scoped to the court and then unscoped; and a search for the exact citation string in other opinions, accepted when a later court cites that volume and page next to the claimed name (cite corroboration). A hit must pass a name test, a year test (the decision year must fit the years of the reporter volume, and the year the answer gave) and a court test. A real case whose known cite in the same reporter differs stays fabricated, as a miscited real case. On 2026-09-22 at 19:00 UTC, check_brief itself began resolving a cite newer than the index by name, decision date and court (resolution "name_year"); the grader counts those as real but unindexed too. All of this is a second route into the same corpus, not an independent source. Beyond it, no fabricated verdict has been checked by hand against an outside source, except the three top frontier rows' in section 4 (Fable 5.1's two and Opus 5.5's one; none is an invented case, and Opus's is a corpus gap). Every citation graded fabricated will be published with the per-citation grades so readers can check it elsewhere.

Contamination. syfert.com pages are public and crawlable. Models may have been trained on them, which could help them in both arms. The question prompts are generated from propositions, not copied from page text, but a proposition may appear nearly verbatim on a case page. Published question sets also leak. [Proposal, not yet decided: hold back a private rotating slice and report whether results on it differ.]

Prompt sensitivity was not tested. Results depend on the template, and the explicit permission to abstain probably lowers fabrication. The planned check, a 50-question subset run with alternative templates including one without the abstention sentence, was not done. The five templates are mixed across the set, but the sample is too small to compare them. This is a limitation of these results.

The runs are not set up identically. Claude Fable 5.1 and Opus 5.5 ran on the Anthropic API; the other hosted models ran through OpenRouter. Output caps and reasoning settings differ by model (section 7), because some models returned empty answers under the defaults. Gemma 4 26B is a community quantized build with a 2,048-token output cap. Empty or errored answers are left out of the graded counts: Sonnet 5 with tools 5 empty; Qwen 3.8 Flash with tools 4 empty and 2 rate-limited; DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1 empty bare and 2 empty with tools; Gemini 3.8 Flash 1 empty bare. The Claude Code harness rows use Claude Code's own agent loop and a different bare system prompt (section 7), so they are grouped separately. Gemini 3.8 Flash with tools is excluded altogether: 37 of its 40 answers failed when the OpenRouter account ran out of credit mid-run (HTTP 402), so its 3 graded answers say nothing about the model.

The author audited the grader. The hand audit in section 3 was done by Graham Syfert, who also built the tools and the grader. An independent reviewer would be better; the audit packet and the verdicts are in the repository (audit/) so anyone can redo it.

Sample size. Each full-length row has 150 questions; most tool rows have 55 to 60 graded answers. That is enough to separate large differences and not small ones. For question-level proportions (gold_recall, abstain_rate, warned_rate) we report Wilson 95% intervals. The half-width near 50% is about 8 points at n = 150 and about 13 points at n = 55; Sonnet 5 with tools, for example, has gold recall 92.7% with an interval of 82.7 to 97.1. For the citation-level rates, citations within one answer are not independent, so the results table's intervals come from a bootstrap that resamples questions (10,000 resamples): Fable 5.1 misgrounded 7.5% (5.6 to 9.5), Gemma 4 26B with tools 2.8% (1.0 to 4.8). The charts use Wilson intervals over citations, which are narrower. warned_rate rests on 18 bad-law questions, and 4 or 5 in the shorter rows, so treat it as rough. Bare-versus-tools deltas are computed on the questions graded in both arms; no formal paired test was run. With 17 rows and several metrics, some differences will look significant by chance; we do not rank rows whose intervals overlap.

Non-determinism and drift. Hosted models change without notice and sample stochastically. Each run records model ID, date and settings in runs.config_json; a result describes that model on that date.

8. Grader versions

The numbers published on 2026-09-22 come from grader g6 (g6-parallel-20260922). Since 2026-09-23 all 2,026 stored grades in results.db, and the 570 of the canary runs on the upgraded server (Gemma 4 26B v2, v2+check, v3; Opus 5.5 Claude Code v2, v2+check; merged into results.db on 2026-09-23, 2,596 rows in all), carry g10 (g10-goldequiv-20260923, below), and report.py flags any mix of versions. The hand audit in section 3 drove g4 and g5; g6 fixed how citations are counted; g7 fixed how quotations are matched; g8 fixed how bare pinpoints are counted; g9 fixed twin citations, withdrawn citations and a loose name-match route; g10 fixed gold_equiv for propositions with an ellipsis.

  • g3 (g3-nameyear-20260922), the first grader used on the paid runs. It added the name-and-year reconciliation in reconcile.py, so a real case missing from the reporter index is counted as unindexed, not fabricated. The audit sample was drawn from g3 grades.
  • g4 (g4-quotefix-20260922), from faults found while building the audit page. A name guard stops initials and short corpus names ("P.T. v. M.S." against "Pt v. Ms") from counting as mismatches. Quotes are searched in every resolved case the answer cites, whatever the name verdict; before, a false name mismatch also made that case's quotes count as absent. The same sentence extracted twice is counted once. Text that is not a case quotation ("quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)", a Westlaw topic name, "(internal quotation marks omitted)") is skipped.
  • g5 (g5-audit-20260922), from the audit verdicts. Single-quoted text inside a double-quoted passage is part of the outer quote. Star pages ("*277") and footnote numbers are stripped before matching. A quote that omits the opinion's internal citations still matches. A span counts only if it is presented as a case quotation, with a citation or attribution verb next to it, so the model's own notes to the user are not graded as quotes. Each quote records which case it was found in and which cases were searched. Absent quotes are sub-labelled paraphrase in quotation marks or fabricated wording, and found quotes verbatim or near; the counts do not change. g5 also counts check_brief's own name-and-year resolutions as unindexed.
  • g6 (g6-parallel-20260922), citation structure. Parallel chains are one authority: reporter cites joined only by commas, semicolons or pincites, up to the year parenthetical or the next case name, and cites check_brief swallowed as pincites of a chain. An unresolved official state reporter cite in the same sentence as a resolved regional cite is its parallel; an unpaired one goes through reconciliation and, failing that, counts as unindexed if its year is before 1950. Subsequent history ("aff'd", "rev'd", "mod", "cert. denied", "review denied", "approved", "quashed") is dropped from every count, which fixes g5's "mod" fault (section 4). Citations the model retracted in the same answer are dropped. A fabricated citation that at least two other courts' opinions repeat is tagged court_propagated_miscite and still counted as fabricated. Across the headline slice this lowered citation counts slightly (Fable 5.1 755 to 746) and fabricated counts more (Fable 3 to 2, Opus 2 to 1, Qwen 3.8 Max 100 to 82, Gemma 4 26B bare 171 to 159). The audit re-score under g6 is unchanged: absent 19 of 21 (90.5%), found 20 of 21 (95.2%), name 6 of 6 (100%).
  • g7 (g7-quotematch-20260923), quotation matching, with the same five rules as the Brief Check library patch bc5. They apply only to a quote the g6 matcher calls absent. Typography is folded and words broken by a line-wrap hyphen are joined, on both sides ("dis-\ncretion"). Bracketed alterations are optional ("[t]he first principle gleaned from the [Steelworkers] Trilogy"). A citation inside a quote splits it like an ellipsis, so a trailing "Id.; see" or an internal parenthetical cite no longer breaks the match. The parts must appear in order in one cited opinion, and a part under 20 words must be verbatim. A quote that matches rule text is labelled rule_text, not absent. The text comes from a fixed list of the most-quoted rule sentences (Fed. R. Civ. P. 56, Fed. R. Evid. 403, MRE 403, Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.510 and others) or from any rule the answer names nearby, fetched with get_statute. Subsequent history ("overruled on other grounds by ...") and text inside a docket or Westlaw citation's parenthetical are labelled skipped_non_quote. A quote whose own citation has no cluster (a fabricated cite, or a docket or Westlaw cite), or that sits in an answer with no resolved cited opinion, is counted as quote_unattributed, not absent, because it cannot be checked against its source. g7 also adds gold_equiv beside gold_recall. It is gold_hit, or a verified cited opinion whose text contains a verbatim run of at least 12 words of the question's proposition. It uses this text rule only: get_citing_cases cannot say which passage a citer cites gold for. The regrade reused each row's stored check_brief body and reconciliation evidence (grade.py --regrade --reuse-check-brief), so only quote counts moved.   • g8 (g8-pinref-20260923), pin references. Some answers give a case's full citation and then, on a separate line, a pinpoint written as a bare citation: "Reporter Citation: 223 So. 2d 100 ... Pinpoint: 223 So. 2d 102". The page in the pinpoint is an interior page of the same opinion, so check_brief cannot resolve it (it is not a first page). It lists the case in did_you_mean, and g7 counted the pinpoint as a second, fabricated citation. That is a mistake in citation form, not an invented case. Under g8, an unresolved citation whose volume and reporter match a resolved citation in the same answer, and whose page comes after that case's first page, is a pin reference to that case. It qualifies in one of two ways: did_you_mean names that same case and the page is at most 150 pages in, or the page is at most 60 pages in and did_you_mean does not put the page inside a different case that starts after the cited one. A pin reference is folded into the case it points to. It is not a citation, not fabricated, and is counted in the new per-row n_pin_reference (report column pin_refs). A citation carrying a case name that shares no party name with the cited case never qualifies. Citations that stay fabricated are now tagged as near misses when the index explains the error. The tag is interior_page when did_you_mean puts the page 1 to 60 pages inside a case the answer does not cite ("477 U.S. 248" for Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, 477 U.S. 242).   • g9 (g9-twins-20260923), three fixes. First, twin citations. Citations with the same volume, reporter and first page are one authority, whatever pin page each carries and whichever route resolved each one. The best outcome among the twins holds for all of them, in this order: resolved, unindexed, pin reference, fabricated. The case that exposed this was Opus 5.5 (Claude Code) with MCP v7 on q0052. The answer cited "412 So. 3d 701" (Gudinas v. State, Fla. 2025), which was counted as unindexed. A sentence later it cited "412 So. 3d 701, 709", which went through reconciliation on its own and was counted as fabricated. The name extractor also now stops at a clause boundary. On that pin cite it had returned "Knight v. State, cites it as \"". It also returns no name for a field label ("Citation: 944 So. 2d 1138"). Before, it matched "Citation:" against cases such as "Weller v. Citation Oil & Gas Corp." Second, withdrawn citations. The g6 retraction rule now also covers "did not verify", "does not verify", "could not verify", "could not confirm", "was unable to verify" or "confirm", "does not appear to exist", "may not exist", "do not rely on", "don't rely on" and "I could not locate" (contractions included). The phrase must be in the citation's own sentence or the clause right after it, with nothing between them but a parenthetical, a pin, punctuation, a short subject such as "it" or "the citation", or the case name. "Did not verify", "does not appear to exist" and "may not exist" count only after the citation: "I did not verify X" says the model did not check X, not that X failed. A phrase aimed at a pin, page or quotation ("could not confirm the pin page") does not count. The withdrawn citation is left out of n_cites and n_fabricated and counted in the retracted column. The case that exposed this was the same run on q0038: "The citation I checked, 317 So. 3d 72, did not verify". Third, a stricter name-match route in reconciliation. A check_citation or search_cases hit can now confirm a citation as a real, unindexed case only if two things hold. (a) The party order must match: "Hill v. State" is not "State v. App. is the Court of Appeals of Georgia and Ga. is the Supreme Court of Georgia; the same split applies to Mich. App. and Mich., Ill. App. and Ill., Pa. Super. and Pa., N.J. Super. and N.J., Cal. App. and Cal., and A.D. and N.Y. U.S. and S. Ct. must be the Supreme Court of the United States, F.3d, F.4th and F. App'x a federal court of appeals, F. Supp. a district court, and F. and F.2d any federal court. A regional reporter accepts any court of a state it covers. A circuit named in the parenthetical ("11th Cir.") must be the hit's circuit; g3's court check only asked that the hit be "a circuit". A court the corpus does not name is not checked. Four propositions contain an ellipsis (q0018, q0057, q0124, q0204), and a run cannot cross one. Under g10 a proposition with an ellipsis (". . .", "...", "…" or "[...]") is split at each one. A cited opinion qualifies when every part of at least 5 words appears in it verbatim, in any order; parts under 5 words are ignored. It also qualifies with a run of at least 12 words inside one part. The literal every-part rule alone would have taken gold_equiv away from 6 rows that were right. Five are q0057, whose proposition opens with its own lead-in ("the commissioner must consider the following in determining a claimant's entitlement to benefits") before the quoted four-factor test that the cited Second Circuit opinions carry word for word. The sixth is Gemma 4 26B with tools v1 on q0204, where the one part left is 21 words long. A proposition without an ellipsis keeps the 12-word rule, or needs to appear whole if it is shorter (none of the 300 is). Both sides get the quote matcher's normalisation: whitespace, curly quotes, line-wrap hyphens, digit tokens. Bracketed alterations in the proposition are optional.
